The devices in your distribution board are the only part of the electrical system whose whole job is to act in the half second when something goes wrong. Havells, Legrand and Schneider Electric all make MCBs and RCCBs that do this reliably, and a flat fitted properly with any of the three is well protected. The differences are in price, range, availability and how easy it is to get a matching piece five years later. Here is how we see them.

## What actually differs

**The standard is the same.** Residential MCBs are made to IS/IEC 60898-1 and RCCBs to IS 12640-1. A 16A C-curve MCB from any of the three trips within the same limits, because the standard defines them. Nobody's MCB "trips faster" in a way a flat would notice.

**Breaking capacity.** This is the largest fault current the MCB can interrupt safely, printed on the front as a number in a rectangle, 6000 or 10000. At a flat's distribution board the possible fault current is normally well inside 6 kA; 10 kA gives extra margin and is what most consultants specify. Economy ranges are usually 6 kA and premium ranges 10 kA, and this is the main thing you are paying for when you move up a range.

**Curves and ratings in stock.** All three make B, C and D curves on paper. In practice counters stock C curve in the common ratings. If you want B curve for lighting circuits, or unusual ratings, order ahead.

**RCCB types.** All three sell 30 mA RCCBs in type AC and type A. Type A is the better choice for a home with inverter air conditioners and washing machines, and it is the item most often out of stock at small counters. Ask for it by name.

**The distribution board.** A DB is more than a box. The busbar, the neutral and earth links and the door quality differ between ranges, and each brand designs its board around its own devices. Buy the board, isolator, RCCBs and MCBs as one brand.

## Which is cheaper?

In broad terms: the economy ranges, Legrand RX³ and Schneider Easy9, are the least expensive; Havells sits in the middle; Legrand DX³ and Schneider Acti9 are the most expensive of the group. On a complete 3BHK board the gap between the cheapest and the dearest of these is noticeable but not large compared with the rest of the electrical bill, because a flat needs only twenty or so devices. This is not the place to save money, and it is also not a place where spending the maximum buys visible benefit.

## Where ABB, Siemens and L&T fit

All three are excellent and appear on most project specifications. ABB and Siemens are priced at the premium end and are more common in commercial and industrial work than in flats. L&T's switchgear business now belongs to Schneider Electric and is being sold under the Lauritz Knudsen name, which explains why older "L&T" boards in Gurgaon societies are now topped up with a differently branded device. If your building's existing boards are one of these makes, staying with that make for additions is usually simplest.

## Do not mix brands in one board

The devices clip to a standard rail, so a mixed board is physically possible, and electricians do it when one rating is out of stock. It is poor practice. The comb busbar is made for one brand's terminal spacing, a mixed board looks improvised, consultants reject it at handover, and each brand will point at the other if something fails. One brand per board.

## Spotting a fake

Protective devices are counterfeited because they look simple. A fake MCB may not trip at all.

1. **Buy sealed, with a GST invoice** from an authorised channel, with the brand and rating on the invoice.
2. **Check the printing:** rating, curve, breaking capacity, standard and ISI mark should be sharp and permanent, not a sticker.
3. **Scan the hologram or QR** on the pack where the brand provides one.
4. **Compare weight and action** with a known genuine piece. Fakes are lighter and the toggle feels loose.
5. **Press the test button** on every RCCB after installation and once every few months after that. It should trip instantly.

## Which to choose

- **Flat owner fitting or replacing a board:** Havells if you want the easiest future availability, or Legrand DX³ or Schneider Acti9 if you prefer a premium range. All in 10 kA, with a type A RCCB.
- **Rental flat or tight budget:** Legrand RX³ or Schneider Easy9. Still a known brand, still ISI-marked.
- **Matching the switches:** if the switches are Legrand or Schneider, taking the DB from the same brand gives you one warranty contact. See our [modular switch comparison](/blog/legrand-vs-schneider-vs-anchor-vs-gm-modular-switches/).

- **Adding to an existing board:** stay with the make already in it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which is the best MCB brand for a home in India?

Havells, Legrand and Schneider Electric are all reliable choices for a flat, and ABB, Siemens and L&T are equally good. There is no single best; choose a genuine 10 kA device from one of them, bought through an authorised dealer, and keep the whole board to one brand.

### Is Havells better than Legrand or Schneider for MCBs?

Not better, but the easiest to find in NCR when you need a replacement or an extra rating. Legrand and Schneider offer both an economy and a premium range and are more often named in consultant specifications. All three protect a flat equally well.

### What is the difference between a 6 kA and a 10 kA MCB?

It is the largest fault current the MCB can safely interrupt. 6 kA is adequate for most flats; 10 kA gives more margin and is what most specifications ask for. The number is printed on the front of the device inside a rectangle.

### Can I use an MCB of one brand in another brand's distribution board?

It will usually fit on the rail, but it should be avoided. The busbar is designed for one brand's devices, a mixed board is rejected on projects, and warranty responsibility becomes unclear. Use the same brand for the board and everything in it.
